\subsection{Pauli Propagation beyond 100 Qubits}
Classical simulation of a quantum circuit stores either the state or the observable.
State vector simulation stores all $2^n$ amplitudes of the state, requiring 16~PiB for 50 qubits in double precision~\cite{haner2017petabyte}.
Tensor network simulation compresses the state into small tensors, and its cost grows with the entanglement of the circuit~\cite{tindall2024efficient}.
Pauli propagation tracks the observable, not the state, as a list of Pauli terms in the Heisenberg picture and computes its expectation value for circuits with more than 100 qubits~\cite{begusic2024fast, rudolph2025pauli}.
This list sets the cost of the simulation, and our work focuses on Pauli propagation, since its cost follows the number of kept terms in place of the number of qubits.

\noindent\textbf{Branch, Merge, and Truncate.}
Each term of the list consists of a coefficient and a Pauli string, and the string maps to a bit vector with an x bit and a z bit for every qubit.
With this bit vector, the product of two strings becomes the XOR of their bit vectors up to a phase.
A rotation gate with generator $Q$ keeps every term that commutes with $Q$ and branches every anticommuting term $P$ into $P$ with $\cos\theta$ and $P \oplus Q$ with $\sin\theta$.
These branches can double the number of terms at every rotation.
The merge then sums the coefficients of the terms with equal strings, and the truncation removes the terms with a coefficient magnitude below the cutoff.
Clifford gates map each string to one string and keep the number of terms, and noise channels scale the coefficients (Figure~\ref{fig:pp}).

\noindent\textbf{Term Growth with Accuracy.}
The cutoff of the truncation sets the accuracy and the number of terms together.
On a 30-qubit circuit with an exact answer, the error decreases from 0.019 at cutoff $10^{-3}$ to 0.00007 at cutoff $10^{-7}$.
On the 127-qubit kicked Ising circuit, the expectation value moves from 0.153 at cutoff $10^{-4}$ to 0.229 at $10^{-5}$ and 0.322 at $10^{-6}$.
The largest boundary count rises to 159 million terms at cutoff $10^{-5}$ and 11.9 billion at $10^{-6}$.
The latter occupies about 475~GB at 40 bytes per term before execution buffers.
Within a window, we also sum the maximum term count reached by each sector execution.
The largest of these sums reaches 1.4 billion and 100 billion at the two cutoffs, respectively.
This counter captures intermediate expansion across sector executions, not a simultaneous memory footprint.

\noindent\textbf{Merge before Truncation.}
The truncation must see the merged coefficient of each string.
Equal strings that arise from different parents carry parts of one coefficient, and each part can lie below the cutoff although their sum exceeds it.
A truncation of these unmerged parts removes this sum and changes the answer.
For example, the 127-qubit kicked Ising circuit at cutoff $10^{-4}$ yields 0.194-0.283 when the truncation sees unmerged parts, whereas the merged run yields 0.1531.
The merge partner of a new term depends on the gate that creates it and not on the location of its parent.
An arbitrary distribution of the parents does not guarantee that their merge partners are local.
A locality-preserving partition must account for the generators that create these partners (Table~\ref{tab:sv_pp}).

These two bottlenecks intensify at scale.
The terms of an accurate run must spread over the GPUs of many nodes, whereas every equal string must meet its merge partners before each truncation.
Existing systems run Pauli propagation on one device~\cite{begusic2024fast, rudolph2025pauli}, merge the terms across all GPUs after every gate~\cite{cupauliprop}, or fix the partition of the strings before the run~\cite{paulistrings}.
A partition that follows both the gates and the live terms across nodes is not available in these systems.

\subsection{Challenges in Distributed Pauli Propagation}
Existing distributed systems merge the terms across all GPUs after every gate or partition the strings by rules fixed before the run~\cite{cupauliprop, orqa, paulistrings}.
We measure the first design with \cuPP~v0.5.0 on the 127-qubit kicked Ising circuit and NVIDIA A100 GPUs, and the measurement exposes two challenges, a global step after every gate and a capacity bound by the GPU memory.

\noindent\textbf{Global Step after Every Gate.}
\cuPP merges the terms of every gate in a global step, since a new term may merge with a term on any GPU.
Each MPI rank applies the gate to its terms and calls allgather 22 times to merge and redistribute the terms with all other ranks, and 57\% of the gates have no term to exchange.
This global step adds a fixed cost of 5.9, 22.5, and 29.8~ms per gate on 4, 8, and 16 GPUs, against 0.05-0.8~ms on one GPU (Figure~\ref{fig:anatomy}).
As a result, a run with 22.5 million terms takes 2.9-3.2~s on one GPU and 33-48, 65, and 71~s on 4, 8, and 16 GPUs.
The GPUs compute during 9\% of this run, and the terms remain evenly spread at 1.001 times the mean on the largest rank.
Thus, the cost of the global step, not an imbalance of the terms, slows the run as GPUs are added.

\noindent\textbf{Capacity Bound by the GPU Memory.}
\cuPP also keeps all terms of every gate in GPU memory.
Within a group of consecutive gates, the number of terms rises to 2.9-36 times its value at the start of the group in the seven circuits, and the merge requires working space next to these terms.
As a result, \cuPP runs out of memory at 88-104 million terms per rank on 1 to 16 GPUs, and these kept terms occupy about 4~GB of the 36~GB that it uses on each GPU.
A deferred merge reduces the global steps, but its unmerged copies exceed the memory of 16 GPUs on two of the seven circuits.
A run without the global merge does not lift this bound and returns a wrong answer, 0.1675 on 4 GPUs and 0.189 on 16 GPUs against 0.1531, since its truncation discards parts of equal strings.

\subsection{Opportunity in Parities Conserved by Windows}
The two challenges require a partition that keeps every merge partner on the GPU of its parent without a global step.
We examine where this partition exists with the generators of the seven circuits and with the terms of the kicked Ising circuit.

\noindent\textbf{Parities Conserved by Windows of Gates.}
A rotation with generator $Q$ changes a term $P$ into $P \oplus Q$, and every parity $r$ with $r \cdot Q = 0$ assigns the same value to both terms.
A group of consecutive gates, a window, conserves every parity orthogonal to all of its generators, and these parities divide the terms into sectors that keep the merge partners together.
We count the windows in the seven circuits under the rule that a window grows while at least $n$ conserved parities remain (Table~\ref{tab:windows}).
The circuits with 1,100-7,960 gates form 8-45 windows, and a partition by window parities moves terms only at the window boundaries, 89-398 times fewer than the gates.
In contrast, the whole circuit conserves at most two parities, and every term keeps the value of the observable on these parities.
Thus, no parity divides the terms for the whole run, and the partition must change between windows.

\noindent\textbf{Fixed Partitions Move Terms.}
Existing partitions fix their parities for the whole run and apply every gate to the terms~\cite{paulistrings}.
Under these partitions, every gate that changes a fixed parity moves terms between processes.
With 16 parts, rows over all bits move terms at 93-96\% of the gates of the seven circuits, and rows over only the x bits or only the z bits move terms at 31-95\% of the gates.
On the kicked Ising circuit, random rows change the owner of 94\% of the terms at a layer of two-qubit gates, and rows over lattice regions reduce this share to 68-89\% with the largest of 16 parts at 1.65-3.37 times the mean.
The library that implements these partitions keeps its rows for the whole run, since a changed row would move the owner of every term, and it lists the automatic choice of rows as an open problem~\cite{paulistrings}.

\noindent\textbf{Partitions that Follow the Run.}
The parities of each window follow its gates, and the balance of the sectors follows the live terms.
On the kicked Ising circuit at cutoff $10^{-5}$, the terms at the start of a window grow from 1 to 159 million over the run, and the first windows hold too few terms to divide (Figure~\ref{fig:windows}).
The terms within a window then rise to 2.9-36 times this start in the seven circuits, and the sectors of a balanced partition differ from window to window.
As a result, only the live terms of the run determine the number of sectors and the parities that balance them.
Thus, distributed Pauli propagation requires a partition that the system recomputes for every window from its gates and from the live terms during the run.

These observations motivate partitions that preserve merge locality across each window and change with the live expansion.
Execution must also separate the stored terms from each GPU's working set to use host memory for capacity.
