\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Evaluation Setup}
We evaluate correctness, execution time, and capacity under explicitly matched inputs and truncation rules.

\noindent\textbf{Hardware Specification.}
All experiments use GPU nodes of the NERSC Perlmutter system.
Each node contains four NVIDIA A100 GPUs with 40~GB of device memory each, a 64-core CPU, and 256~GiB of host memory.
The nodes communicate over the Slingshot network.
GPU implementations use a memory budget of 90\% of the available device memory, approximately 36~GB per GPU.
\SectorQ assigns one MPI rank to each node and one worker to each GPU.
The distributed mode of \cuPP assigns one rank to each GPU.
The scale experiments use up to 128 nodes and 512 GPUs, while the single-node experiments compare each implementation on the resources it supports.
CPU implementations use the same nodes as the corresponding GPU configuration, with their process and thread counts stated below.
The comparisons therefore identify both the number of nodes and the execution devices.

\noindent\textbf{Evaluation Scope.}
The evaluation measures Heisenberg-picture propagation that returns an observable expectation value.
A coefficient cutoff controls the retained operator size, and lower cutoffs usually require more terms.
Unless stated otherwise, each setting is measured once with a 40-minute limit.
The size and exact-reference experiments use a 20-minute limit, and the capacity ladders allow \SectorQ up to two hours per run.
A ladder stops after memory exhaustion or its time limit, and an unexecuted point is not a measured failure.
Reported times cover the propagation region rather than batch queue or process-launch delays.
Initialization performed lazily inside that region remains included.
Term counts on the horizontal axes denote the largest count at a \SectorQ window boundary under the stated truncation rule.
These counts differ from the larger within-window population and from the final output size.
Separate experiments measure repeated-run variability, preserving the single-run policy for the main sweeps.

\noindent\textbf{Circuits.}
The seven circuits in Table~\ref{tab:windows} cover different interaction graphs, gate families, and observables.
The principal workload is a 127-qubit heavy-hex kicked Ising circuit with 20 steps and rotation angle $\pi/4$.
A second heavy-hex circuit uses transverse-field Ising model (TFIM) rotations at general angles, and a noisy variant adds depolarizing channels with probability 0.005.
The remaining circuits are a TFIM on an $11\times11$ lattice, an XXZ model on an $8\times8$ lattice, a 100-qubit QAOA circuit on a random regular graph, and a 100-qubit hardware-efficient ansatz.
Their respective depths are 10 steps, 6 steps, 4 layers, and 16 layers.
The observables include a local magnetization and the edge sum of QAOA.
Additional TFIM grids and kicked Ising depths test whether the behavior extends beyond the main circuit sizes.
All comparisons within a setting preserve its circuit, observable, and initial state.

\noindent\textbf{Baselines.}
The primary baseline is \cuPP~v0.5.0 with the original circuit and its default distributed mode.
Our second baseline uses a Clifford-frame input and defers global deduplication until truncation, with additional merges where required to fit memory.
We report the fastest successful deferred interval among those tested.
The fixed-partition comparison uses paulistrings-rs with the original gates and matches its gate-level truncation rule.
Other comparisons include the authors' SPD, MonoProp, PauliPropagation.jl, and ppvm implementations, Qiskit's Pauli propagation package, and our reproduction of ORQA.
These external implementations, except paulistrings-rs, receive the exported Clifford-frame operation list.
ORQA uses 64 ranks per node, MonoProp uses eight ranks with eight threads each, and the threaded single-node implementations use 64 threads.
Qiskit uses one thread.
MonoProp's truncation rule differs, and its timings are not treated as comparisons at identical numerical error.
The ORQA results describe our reproduction rather than the authors' implementation.

\subsection{Single-Node Performance}
The single-node comparison establishes the arithmetic and memory behavior before inter-node exchange becomes necessary.

\noindent\textbf{Comparison with GPU Implementations.}
Figure~\ref{fig:single} compares implementations on one node as the kicked Ising cutoff decreases.
On problems that fit one GPU, the measured ratio of the \cuPP single-GPU time to the \SectorQ single-GPU time ranges from 0.6 to 1.5 across circuits.
Both systems use the same library arithmetic, and the differences include circuit preprocessing and sector management.
SPD is up to 2.9 times faster on one GPU in the tested settings, but its kicked Ising run exhausts memory at cutoff $10^{-5}$.
With four GPUs, \SectorQ completes kicked Ising at cutoff $3\times10^{-6}$ in 436~s, whereas the primary distributed baseline stops at a higher cutoff.
At their shared cutoff $3\times10^{-5}$, the times are 5.25 and 44.3~s.
The single-node gain therefore combines parallel execution with a capacity beyond the GPU-resident expansion of the baseline.

\noindent\textbf{Comparison with CPU Implementations.}
The same-node comparisons also identify where CPU execution remains competitive.
At the largest shared settings across circuits, \SectorQ on four GPUs is 108-269 times faster than PauliPropagation.jl, 157-755 times faster than ppvm, and more than 1,800 times faster than the single-threaded Qiskit implementation.
These ratios compare the available execution resources of each implementation rather than identical processor types.
The ORQA reproduction is 18-71 times slower on the same node.
MonoProp is faster on several small settings that finish within two seconds, including kicked Ising at cutoff $10^{-3}$.
Its different truncation policy also requires a separate accuracy comparison.
As the operator grows, \SectorQ retains a larger executable problem range by keeping inactive sectors in host memory.
Thus, the relevant benefit is the joint time and capacity behavior over the cutoff ladder, with the small-problem CPU results retained in the comparison.

\subsection{Performance across Circuits on 16 GPUs}
We next test whether the window construction benefits different circuit structures on the same resources.

\noindent\textbf{Simulation Time.}
Figure~\ref{fig:circuits} compares seven circuits on the same four nodes and 16 GPUs.
At the largest setting completed by the primary \cuPP baseline for each circuit, \SectorQ is 4.4-9.7 times faster.
For kicked Ising at cutoff $10^{-5}$, the original-circuit baseline takes 95.0~s and \SectorQ takes 12.2~s.
The comparison with deferred deduplication separates the benefit of fewer global merges from the benefit of window sectors.
That baseline remains 2.2-4.6 times slower at the shared settings and exhausts memory in two circuits at the primary baseline's largest completed setting.
\SectorQ also completes lower cutoffs in all seven circuits.
The gain appears both in circuits with Clifford gates and in TFIM and XXZ circuits whose input contains no removable Clifford gates.
Consequently, the observed improvement extends beyond the reduced operation count of the Clifford frame.

\noindent\textbf{Time per Term.}
Figure~\ref{fig:cost} divides propagation time by the largest boundary term count of the setting.
This normalization measures time per retained term rather than the duration of a single gate application, since a term may participate in many operations.
For settings above 100 million boundary terms, \SectorQ takes 17-130~ns per term across the circuits.
The curve becomes relatively flat as the arithmetic on large sectors dominates the fixed cost of library calls and window coordination.
At cutoff $10^{-4}$, the original-circuit \cuPP baseline takes 908-39,000~ns per term.
Its global operations retain substantial cost even when few terms survive.
The difference decreases as a baseline run contains more useful arithmetic, consistent with the smaller speedups at its largest successful settings.
The curves therefore connect the time comparisons to problem size without assuming identical work per term across different circuits.

\noindent\textbf{Agreement of Answers.}
The completed runs with a common truncation rule agree across implementations and GPU counts.
Across 68 circuit-cutoff settings with at least two successful runs, the largest absolute difference in the expectation value is $4.9\times10^{-11}$.
The comparison includes the single-GPU reference, distributed \cuPP, and external implementations with the same coefficient decisions.
The largest absolute difference occurs for QAOA, whose observable is an edge sum rather than one local Pauli expectation.
\SectorQ also preserves the printed answer when the number of sectors, GPU count, or window rule changes.
These checks test the numerical consequence of merge locality through actual partition and exchange operations.
They do not establish that a chosen cutoff approximates the exact quantum expectation sufficiently well.
Section~\ref{sec:accuracy} separately compares a smaller circuit with an exact state-vector reference and reports the cutoff dependence of the larger kicked Ising workload.

\subsection{Comparison with Fixed Linear Partitions}

\noindent\textbf{Same Truncation Rule.}
Figure~\ref{fig:prs} compares \SectorQ with paulistrings-rs on 16 GPUs using truncation after each operation to match its gate-level coefficient decisions.
Both implementations receive the same original circuit, and the fixed partition uses the library's seeded rows.
Across 75 completed settings on one, four, and 16 GPUs, the expectation values agree within $4.7\times10^{-11}$.

At the next tested cutoff in five circuits, paulistrings-rs exhausts GPU memory while \SectorQ completes in 29-880~s.
Small-setting timings include any lazy GPU kernel compilation in the propagation call.
The comparison uses the implementation's fixed partitions and distinguishes their time and capacity from the within-engine ablation below.

\subsection{Circuit Size and Depth}
Additional sweeps vary the circuit dimensions beyond the main seven-circuit suite.

\noindent\textbf{Number of Qubits.}
Figure~\ref{fig:size} varies a two-dimensional TFIM lattice from 36 to 1,024 qubits and propagates its total magnetization.
This experiment changes the string length, graph size, and observable size together, rather than changing only the coefficient cutoff.
At 1,024 qubits on a $32\times32$ lattice, \SectorQ on 16 GPUs finishes in 90.4~s and is 4.1 times faster than the primary \cuPP baseline.
The implementation extends the binary generator and row operations to the wider strings required by these lattices.
The largest qubit count is the supported library limit in this experiment, not a measured limit on the mathematical partition rule.
As in the main sweep, implementations that reject the qubit count are distinguished from executions that exceed memory or time.
The result shows that the sector construction applies beyond the 127-qubit heavy-hex workload used for the largest capacity runs.

\noindent\textbf{Circuit Depth.}
The depth experiment keeps the kicked Ising graph fixed and increases the number of Trotter steps.
Longer propagation changes both the number of windows and the population of terms encountered within them.
At 60 steps, \SectorQ on 16 GPUs completes in 160~s, 50 times faster than the original-circuit \cuPP distributed baseline.
The same window construction is applied throughout the deeper circuit without a partition chosen for the final operator in advance.
The curve in Figure~\ref{fig:size} therefore measures the combined cost of additional operations, changing term counts, and repeated boundary exchanges.
A deeper circuit does not imply a proportional increase in retained terms, since truncation can remove branches introduced earlier.
We consequently compare the measured times at each depth rather than extrapolate from the operation count alone.
The result supports the execution mechanism over longer sequences of windows as well as larger graphs.

\subsection{Extreme Scale-out}
The capacity experiments lower the kicked Ising cutoff until execution or host storage limits the next step.

\noindent\textbf{Largest Finished Problems.}
The kicked Ising capacity ladder reaches 448.2 billion boundary terms on 512 GPUs at cutoff $1.5\times10^{-7}$.
This expansion occupies 17.9~TB at 40 bytes per term and completes in 3,413~s.
The original-circuit \cuPP baseline on the same 512 GPUs finishes at cutoff $2\times10^{-6}$, corresponding to 3.2 billion boundary terms, and fails at the next tested cutoff.
The completed problem is therefore 140 times larger by boundary term count.
This ratio compares \SectorQ specifically with \cuPP, not with all CPU implementations.
On 128 GPUs, \SectorQ completes 117.5 billion terms, while the 256-GPU endpoint remains under remeasurement after a stalled run.
We retain its completed results without declaring the unresolved next step a memory limit.
The capacity comparisons also distinguish measured failures from larger settings rejected because their retained terms alone exceed installed host memory.

\noindent\textbf{Host Memory Use.}
The 512-GPU capacity run allocates 21,079~GiB in host chunks across 128 nodes, 64\% of their installed host memory.
This counter measures the chunk pool, not the entire resident memory of every process.
The ratio to the boundary expansion includes reusable chunks and the transient storage needed for execution and exchange.
The round-based transfer prevents the node from reserving a complete second receive expansion before it releases sent chunks.
For the largest run, this representation supports a stored operator substantially larger than aggregate GPU memory while each worker processes a bounded sector.
The next cutoff on the ladder is not reported as a completed or measured out-of-memory run when its footprint is only estimated.
Host capacity remains a practical limit, and a single oversized sector must still admit a valid finer partition that fits the GPU working buffers.

\subsection{Accuracy}
Numerical validation separates the distributed implementation from the approximation introduced by truncation.
\label{sec:accuracy}

\noindent\textbf{Exact Reference.}
Figure~\ref{fig:accuracy} uses a 30-qubit TFIM on a $5\times6$ lattice with an exact state-vector reference.
The reference calculation was checked against Pauli propagation without truncation on smaller grids.
The four-step circuit reaches absolute error $7.2\times10^{-5}$ at cutoff $10^{-7}$, with \SectorQ on 16 GPUs completing in 29.2~s.
Methods with the same truncation rule produce the same error at a given cutoff, making their time comparison meaningful at that error.
MonoProp has a separate error because its coefficient decisions differ.
The six-step circuit requires substantially more work and reaches the time or memory limit at higher cutoffs.
Neither sweep is strictly monotonic in error at every intermediate cutoff.
The results therefore establish the cost of specific numerical approximations against a known answer, rather than a general error bound implied by the cutoff.

\noindent\textbf{Convergence of Kicked Ising.}
The large kicked Ising workload lacks an exact reference at the evaluated scale.
Its expectation values at successively lower cutoffs from $10^{-6}$ to $1.5\times10^{-7}$ are approximately 0.3217, 0.3256, 0.3265, 0.3226, 0.3153, and 0.3089.
These values span about 0.018 and are not monotonic.
A lower cutoff therefore cannot be presented as a measured guarantee of smaller physical error for this circuit.
The scalability result is that \SectorQ computes the same truncated propagation as the matched baselines where both finish and extends the computation to lower cutoffs with more retained terms.
The distinction matters because the capacity improvement makes larger approximations accessible without proving their convergence.
The exact-reference experiment and the cross-implementation agreement address complementary questions: approximation error in a tractable circuit and faithful distributed execution of a specified truncation rule.

\subsection{Sensitivity to Parameters}
Figure~\ref{fig:knobs} varies sector size, tasks per GPU, and the available GPU memory.

\noindent\textbf{Sector Size and Device Memory.}
At cutoff $10^{-5}$, reducing the target sector size to one quarter of the default increases time by 9-26\%, whereas doubling it reduces time by 2-9\% across the representative circuits.
More sectors increase the number of library calls, while larger sectors approach the buffer limit earlier during a window.
One to four tasks per GPU differ by at most 9\% in these measurements, and eight tasks can increase time by up to 35\% on 16 GPUs.
A separate device-memory experiment reduces the budget from 36 to 4~GB with unchanged expectation values.
The smallest budget increases time by 12-64\% but still completes the tested settings through finer sector execution.
These results support the separation of stored capacity from per-GPU working memory.
They do not imply that one sector cap is optimal at every cluster size, as the 512-GPU decomposition demonstrates.
A separate 512-GPU kicked Ising run at cutoff $3\times10^{-6}$ with a cap of 4,096 sectors and smaller host chunks takes 11.6~s, compared with 9.7~s under the default configuration.
This joint parameter change does not isolate the cap, but it shows that greater available concurrency alone need not improve the measured time.

\subsection{Performance Variability}
Figure~\ref{fig:variability} reports ten repetitions of representative kicked Ising settings.

\noindent\textbf{Repeated Runs.}
The repetition experiment contains 160 completed runs over problem sizes and GPU counts.
We define spread as the maximum minus minimum time divided by the mean of ten runs.
On 16 GPUs, the spread is 1.4-7.2\% for \SectorQ and 3.4-7.6\% for the original-circuit \cuPP baseline.
All repeated runs preserve the printed expectation value.
Each system's 16-GPU repetitions use one allocation, but the two systems use different allocations.
This choice avoids mixing allocation changes into a system's repeated-run spread while retaining that distinction in the comparison.
At cutoff $10^{-5}$, the ratio of the ten-run means is 7.6, close to the main sweep's single-run ratio of 7.8.
The repetition results therefore support the reported time difference at this setting without treating a single measurement as a confidence interval.
